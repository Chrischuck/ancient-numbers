# Calculator reasoning: sources and scope

Translate explains how the selected tradition writes, names, or physically represents the entered quantity. Calculate teaches only the selected operation, using the entered operands. Base conversion has a separate `/bases/` page. A translated equation remains available in a collapsed section below the method.

All 40 calculators provide worked numerical lessons for addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division: 160 combinations, including binary and hexadecimal. Each lesson uses the selected operation and entered operands. Native signs, words, or gestures illustrate supported intermediate quantities and results; an evidence notice never substitutes for the numerical walkthrough.

Non-decimal working stays in its own radix or named grouping system. Babylonian lessons combine sexagesimal table facts; binary and hex carry in their own digits; vigesimal lessons exchange twenty-based groups; Maya component products use dots and bars; Mangarevan and Yorùbá captions keep named groups; Ethiopic captions retain their signs. Decimal equivalents appear in separate small hints, including the main calculator result caption. Conversion expansions remain in Translate and on the Bases page. Each calculation step has its own line or paragraph.

The calculation lessons distinguish documented practices, reconstructions, modern examples, and recording notation used after a separate calculation. The evidence label is visible beside the relevant lesson. Number-name morphology is not treated as a universal description of a speaker’s mental arithmetic. When no historical recipe was established, the calculator still supplies an explicitly reconstructed or modern worked example without attributing it to the historical community.

These audits record the inspected sources, searches, tool distinctions, operation-level findings, and limits. The corresponding `src/data/arithmetic-*.ts` profiles supply source links and four separate operation notes to the calculator.

- [Africa](arithmetic-research-africa.md): 11 systems, including Rhind completion, Bamum palace tables, Coptic manuscript tables, Rumi complementation, Ethiopic halving/doubling, and observed Yorùbá shell grouping.
- [Asia](arithmetic-research-asia.md): 10 systems, including Sunzi rod arithmetic, Suzhou off-book abacus work, Tamil recited tables, and the distinction between written accounting forms and calculating tools.
- [Americas and Pacific](arithmetic-research-americas-pacific.md): 11 systems, including Maya product-grid reconstruction, Kaktovik classroom arithmetic, Khipu recording with reconstructed kernel workspaces, and Mangarevan mixed grouping.
- [Europe](arithmetic-research-europe.md): 6 systems, including Eutocius’ letter-value products, Roman/Greek counter-board reconstruction, Cistercian recording, and attested hand/time-sign representations.

The remaining two systems are modern binary and hexadecimal, with explicit base-two/base-sixteen arithmetic. The About page provides links to all 160 operator lessons. Coptic division links use a surviving table divisor; other divisors change the lesson’s evidence label to reconstruction.

## Calculation tools in the walkthrough

Each tool-based step pairs the previous and resulting quantities, highlights changed columns, and explains the move. Filled pieces are counted; outlined pieces are inactive. Accessible descriptions include active bead/counter counts and place weights. Mobile layouts stack the two states.

- **Suzhou:** a Chinese suanpan with two upper beads worth five and five lower beads worth one per decimal rod. Addition/subtraction use five/ten complements. Multiplication and division use separated, labeled working states as a teaching adaptation; Suzhou numerals record the quantities rather than functioning as abacus beads.
- **Kaktovik:** the documented Iñupiaq arrangement has three upper five-beads and four lower unit beads per base-twenty column. Addition, subtraction, division, and the modern multiplication extension show changes on that tool as well as Kaktovik numeral forms.
- **Roman/Greek/Cistercian:** simplified decimal counter boards are labeled reconstructions, rather than replicas of one uniquely established historical layout. A Cistercian cipher records the resulting quantity; its intersecting strokes are not treated as an arithmetic algorithm.
- **Khipu:** a reconstructed decimal kernel workspace shows calculation separately from the knot record. The lesson states that competing yupana interpretations do not establish one authentic cell layout or set of weights.
- **Written work:** modern scratch boxes distinguish working quantities from Rumi, Sinhala, Siyaq, or Cherokee recording forms where necessary.

## Textbook model for worked explanations

[OpenStax, Contemporary Mathematics 4.2: Early Numeration Systems](https://openstax.org/books/contemporary-mathematics/pages/4-2-early-numeration-systems) works through actual numeral groups: identify each group, give its value, assign a place value where needed, and add the contributions. Its Roman examples identify letters and subtractive pairs rather than giving an unexplained sum. Its Maya examples assign increasing powers of 20 from bottom to top. This informs the panel’s connection between the rendered numeral and its numerical reading.

[OpenStax 4.3: Converting with Base Systems](https://openstax.org/books/contemporary-mathematics/pages/4-3-converting-with-base-systems) teaches conversion from decimal with repeated division, retaining each remainder, then reading the remainders in reverse order. Converting to Maya notation has two stages: find base-20 digits, then write those digits with the appropriate signs. The panel should make both stages visible. Example 4.24 contains editorial inconsistencies in its numbers and vertical ordering; the app uses the correct bottom-to-top place order described in section 4.2 and verifies its own arithmetic.

[Pearson, New General Mathematics 1 teacher’s guide, Chapter 1](https://za.pearson.com/content/dam/region-growth/south-africa/pearson-south-africa/TeacherResourceMaterial/9781447978329_ngm_mat_fm1_tg_eng_ng_screen.pdf), printed pages 13–14 (PDF pages 25–26), separates counting in groups, written symbols, counter-board operations, and place value. That distinction supports treating a numeral’s construction separately from evidence for a historical calculating method.

The resulting design rules are specific to this app:

- Start with the selected notation’s rule and apply it to the input. A decimal decomposition belongs only where it explains that notation’s actual construction.
- Name the visible signs, groups, rows, or quadrants. A formula alone does not tell a learner which marks to draw or how to read them.
- Keep zero places in positional explanations. State what an empty cell or zero sign preserves, even though its contribution to the final sum is zero.
- Finish with a check that reconstructs the original value. Omit steps that merely repeat the input or result.
- Keep historical qualifications near the relevant method. General scope notes should not displace the worked explanation.

Original worked checks for the implementation:

- **Egyptian 56:** five signs worth 10 and six signs worth 1; check `5 × 10 + 6 × 1 = 56`. For 3,056, use three 1,000 signs, five 10 signs, and six 1 signs; no hundred sign is required.
- **Roman 56:** `L = 50`, `V = 5`, and `I = 1`, so write `LVI`. For 349, write `CCC`, `XL`, and `IX`; check `300 + (50 − 10) + (10 − 1) = 349`.
- **Babylonian 56:** one base-60 digit, made from five ten-wedges and six unit-wedges. For 3,656, the digit groups are `1, 0, 56`; check `1 × 3,600 + 0 × 60 + 56 × 1 = 3,656`. Retain the middle place in the app’s reading grid.
- **Maya 56:** `56 = 2 × 20 + 16`; put two dots in the twenties row and three bars plus one dot in the units row below. For 416, the rows from top to bottom are `1, 0, 16`; check `1 × 400 + 0 × 20 + 16 × 1 = 416`. The middle shell preserves the twenties row.

## Sources used for distinct approaches

- **Egyptian doubling and division through multiplication:** [Arnold Buffum Chace’s translation of the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, volume I, page 4](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Rhind_Mathematical_Papyrus,_Volume_I.pdf/20) documents selected doubling rows and multiplication-based division. [Open University’s Egyptian calculation lesson](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/mathematics-statistics/egyptian-mathematics/content-section-1.1.2) supplies context, including unit fractions. The worked table is a modern application; it does not reproduce the papyrus’s hieratic text using the exhibit’s hieroglyphs.
- **Egyptian addition and subtraction:** addition collects like signs and exchanges ten for one of the next value as a teaching reconstruction. Subtraction instead completes the smaller quantity to the larger using the documented notion of a deficit in [Chace’s Rhind translation, page 13](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:The_Rhind_Mathematical_Papyrus,_Volume_I.pdf/29). The worked completion is a new numerical application. The papyrus’s hieratic writing is distinguished from the exhibit’s hieroglyphic illustrations.
- **Chinese decimal rod boards:** [J. J. O’Connor and E. F. Robertson, Chinese numerals](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Chinese_numerals/) describes decimal rod places. [Sunzi Suanjing I §8](https://yawnoc.github.io/sun-tzu/i/8) and [I §9](https://yawnoc.github.io/sun-tzu/i/9), in Conway’s translation, supply multiplication and division procedures with upper, middle, and lower board rows. New operands are worked teaching applications of those instructions.
- **Babylonian multiplication:** [Duncan Melville’s Mesopotamian Mathematics: Multiplication](https://myslu.stlawu.edu/~dmel/mesomath/multiply.html) describes the standard tablet layout: multiples 1–20, then 30, 40, and 50, for selected principal numbers. Larger multipliers combine those entries and sexagesimal place shifts. The lesson does not claim that every number from 1 through 59 had a standard table.
- **Babylonian reciprocal division:** [Open University, Babylonian mathematics](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=1976&printable=1) discusses reciprocal-table evidence and finite versus nonterminating sexagesimal expansions. The application multiplies by an exact reciprocal. Separators, explicit scale, and six-place fractional previews are modern aids; an ellipsis never means the displayed reciprocal is a finite exact value.
- **Roman counter regrouping:** [Oxford Classical Dictionary, Abacus](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.1) describes substitution and Roman-style devices. The panel distinguishes written subtractive numeral groups from a modern exercise with numerical counter columns.
- **Maya positional structure and its limits:** [Berenice Rojo-Garibaldi, Costanza Rangoni, Diego L. González, and Julyan H. E. Cartwright (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8022160/) discuss Mesoamerican positional representations, the Long Count’s mixed multipliers, and the limits of evidence for ordinary vigesimal use. Multiplication follows [Ramírez Alfonsín and Testard’s reconstructed dot/bar product grid](https://imag.umontpellier.fr/~ramirez/Article-maya.pdf): dot×dot = 1, dot×bar = 5, and bar×bar = 25, regrouped as a next-row dot and a current-row bar. All four lessons are labeled reconstructions; no general surviving ancient recipe is asserted. The Long Count’s third place is 360, while the app’s ordinary base-20 layout uses 400.
- **Kaktovik classroom arithmetic:** the [Unicode encoding proposal](https://www.unicode.org/L2/L2021/21058-kaktovik-numerals.pdf) describes graphical addition, subtraction by removing strokes, and division by recognizing groups in the numeral shapes. These are modern classroom practices, not ancient procedures.
- **Oksapmin addition and subtraction:** [Geoffrey Saxe (2015)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/02103702.2015.1054669) documents adaptations of the body sequence for arithmetic in the context of currency and schooling. The lesson coordinates movement along body positions with a separate count of the moves. Subtraction follows the observed example 16 − 7: remove positions 16 through 10 and leave position 9. This is a contemporary ethnographic method, with the demonstration bounded to positions 1–27.
- **Yorùbá number-name formation and counting:** [Matthew Ajibade, A construction morphology approach to Yoruba numerals (2023)](https://constructions.journals.hhu.de/article/download/549/585/2271) informs the shared `yorubaConstruction` function, including 56 = 60 − 4 and 54 = (3 × 20 − 10) + 4. Addition instead demonstrates observed shell batches of five and twenty, with the actual counting evidence linked in the Africa audit. Subtraction, multiplication, and division are explicit reconstructions using supported quantity groups; linguistic complements alone do not establish a universal mental procedure. [Fábùnmi’s comparative dialect study (2010)](https://bop.unibe.ch/linguistik-online/article/download/411/652?inline=1) also demonstrates why dialect variation matters.
- **Mangarevan binary steps:** [Andrea Bender and Sieghard Beller (2013), Mangarevan invention of binary steps for easier calculation](https://math.hawaii.edu/~tom/ethnomathematics/PNAS-2013.pdf), DOI 10.1073/pnas.1309160110, analyzes the 10/20/40/80 grouping vocabulary and demonstrates possible operations. The panel calls this an analytical reconstruction, matching the authors’ distinction between recorded vocabulary and unavailable observations of historical performance. Counting units remain separate from individual objects.
- **Trappist time gestures:** [Du Bois’s 1824 sign list, reproduced in French with Rebecca Lucas’s translation](https://www.medieval-baltic.us/trappe.pdf), entry _Heure_, records fingers for hours and fractional finger/joint references for halves and quarters. The time context supplies a unit; the app does not infer a general cardinal arithmetic algorithm or equate this convention with the written Cistercian cipher.

Other entries inherit their source links and documented rules from their system definitions. Bede’s decimal hand convention, written Cistercian quadrants, additive sign values, multiplier notation, decimal knots, and selected verbal dictionaries are treated as their own representational structures. The Oksapmin body sequence is not described as positional base 27. Example-only dictionaries are never extrapolated into invented words or glyphs.

## Implementation bounds

`renderMathReasoning(system, value, calculation?, glyphs?)` renders escaped HTML and filters source URLs to HTTP(S). `numeral-lesson.ts` connects individual written signs to their values; `spoken-lesson.ts` handles productive decimal number words, and `worked-spoken-lesson.ts` handles the mixed counting units and body path. The calculator and system pages supply the regional artwork to illustrate each step. Detailed source notes are collapsed below the worked example; consequential evidence labels remain visible. Exact arithmetic remains in `numbers.ts`.

- Number formation expands at most twelve decimal digits; larger numbers retain a concise structural description.
- General worked operations use nonnegative whole-number operands through 999,999,999. Long Egyptian doubling tables show their first and last rows, explicitly abbreviating middle rows while retaining the exact sum of all selected rows. All worked lists have at most fourteen steps.
- Signed and fractional inputs retain exact modern results and distinguish absent app artwork from historical mathematical capabilities.
- The Cistercian gesture explanation can describe quarter-hour fractions even though its artwork illustrates whole hours.
- Huge integers are abbreviated only inside the explanatory panel. They are not converted to floating-point numbers or used for unbounded repeated-sign loops.

`tests/math-methods.test.ts` checks numeral formation, actual operation steps and results, reciprocal precision labels, source-linked rendering for every system, historical scope distinctions, HTML escaping, and bounded handling of large or unsupported inputs. `tests/arithmetic-coverage.test.ts` requires all systems × all operators to produce numerical steps and native illustrations, and independently checks manuscript examples and exchange boundaries. Workspace, Maya grid, and Mangarevan tests check their own arithmetic invariants. Browser coverage visits every operator route and checks tool states, narrow layouts, and accessibility.
